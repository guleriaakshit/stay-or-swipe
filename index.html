A research guide for short-form video

Stay or Swipe

What peer-reviewed research says about keeping people watching YouTube Shorts, TikTok and Reels, from the first frame to the last.

  • ModerateA controlled experiment on a close decision: zapping, skipping, viewing time.
  • IndirectA different outcome (likes, clicks, shares) or a distant setting.

No study here measured the Shorts, TikTok or Reels feed directly. The closest are TV zapping and YouTube ad skipping, where the decision to stop watching was measured second by second. Treat the numbers as directions and change one thing at a time.

before you post

What the feed rewards

Watch time and satisfaction, not clicks Indirect

YouTube's own engineers trained its ranking model on expected watch time, choosing it over click-through rate because ranking by clicks "often promotes deceptive videos" [Covington et al. 2016]. A later version also weighs satisfaction signals such as likes and dismissals [Zhao et al. 2019].

A title that wins the click and loses the viewer works against you.

Reach comes from one big broadcast Indirect

For video, reach tracked "the size of the largest broadcast", and more than 99% of shares went no further than one step [Goel et al. 2016]. On a platform, the biggest broadcaster is the recommender.

Spikes fade, momentum compounds Indirect

Socially shared videos rose and fell faster, and videos that stayed popular long-term were rarely viral [Broxton et al. 2013]. About 90% of ~5 million videos never had a burst at all [Crane & Sornette 2008]. Prior views were the strongest predictor of new views, and a channel's existing views explained 64% of a new upload's early views [Borghol et al. 2012].

Expect luck Moderate

Visible popularity made music downloads unequal and unpredictable. The best songs rarely did badly and the worst rarely did well, but anything in between could happen [Salganik et al. 2006].

Judge a format by the median of several videos, never by one.

Titles and thumbnails can be fixed after posting Indirect

Changing a title, thumbnail or keywords after upload raised views about half the time (52%, 53.3%, 50%), against 35% with no change. Title changes lifted search traffic ×1.24 and thumbnail changes lifted related-video traffic ×1.20 [Hoiles et al. 2017]. The sample was mostly gaming channels.

00:00.05

The first glance

Appeal is decided in 50 milliseconds Moderate

Appeal ratings after 50 ms correlated r = .95 with ratings after 500 ms. Appeal tracked how interesting, well designed and well coloured something looked, not how simple or complex it was [Lindgaard et al. 2006]. Impressions formed in 39 ms matched those formed in 1.7 seconds, carried by coarse shapes rather than fine detail [Bar et al. 2006]. In a fast stream of pictures, people caught the gist at 13 ms per picture, but kept details only of pictures whose gist they caught [Potter et al. 2013].

Make the big shapes do the work.

One thing to look at Strong

When everyone's gaze converges on one spot, fewer people leave [Teixeira et al. 2012]. Scattered attention was a bigger cause of zapping than branding [Teixeira et al. 2010]. Faces and text pull the first look, even when people try to avoid them [Cerf et al. 2009]. A saturated object drew the first look 60% of the time and held the eye longer [Hagtvedt & Brasel 2017]. In free viewing, motion predicted where people looked better than brightness did [Mital et al. 2011].

Give every frame one obvious focal point. No collage.

Put it in the centre Moderate

When people skim, the centre wins. During fast-forwarding, the middle 5% of the screen held about 42% of gaze, and a brand placed there was looked at 55% of the time against 3% off-centre [Brasel & Gips 2008]. Rising suspense pulls attention further toward the centre [Bezdek et al. 2015].

Low clutter, strong design Strong

In 249 magazine ads tracked with eye cameras, raw clutter hurt attention to the brand (−.473) and attitude (−.318). Designed structure helped attention to the picture (.682) and comprehension (.400) [Pieters et al. 2010]. In TV ads, both too-simple and too-busy frames raised zapping [Teixeira et al. 2012] [Teixeira et al. 2010]. In 43,978 Instagram posts, fine detail raised likes while a spread-out composition lowered them [Sharma & Peng 2024].

Few raw details, one deliberate composition. A bare card is as risky as a busy one.

Look professional Indirect

Picture quality was the one image effect that held across both platforms tested. Screenshots cost 31–75% of engagement [Li & Xie 2019].

Commit to a colour temperature Moderate

In short videos, a near-neutral colour cast did worst on clicks and sales, while clearly warm or clearly cold colour did best, most of all at the video's peak moment [Wen et al. 2025]. Brightness drives pleasure and saturation drives arousal [Valdez & Mehrabian 1994]. As hues on their own, blue was liked most and yellow among the least in two studies [Valdez & Mehrabian 1994] [Camgöz et al. 2002]. Calm, light colours made a wait feel shorter [Gorn et al. 2004]. Colour meanings are widely shared: across 30 nations, yellow, orange and pink went with joy, black and grey with sadness and fear, and red with both love and anger [Jonauskaite et al. 2020]. In brand ratings, red read as exciting, blue as competent, black as sophisticated and white as sincere [Labrecque & Milne 2012].

Choose the peak frame's colour for the feeling you want, and commit to it.

Text readable in a glance Moderate

Dark text on a light background was never worse. In a dark room, light-on-dark text needed 107–122 ms to read against 83–92 ms for dark-on-light, and in bright light larger letters read faster [Dobres et al. 2017]. Dark-on-light also won at any room brightness [Buchner & Baumgartner 2007] and was rated the most readable pairing [Hall & Hanna 2004]. An open, humanist typeface read faster than a square one, and shrinking the text hurt the square face far more [Dobres et al. 2016]. Across 56 colour pairs, brightness contrast explained almost all of legibility: yellow with black, either way round, ranked near the top, and yellow with white was the worst pairing [Humar et al. 2008].

Dark text on a light background, large, in an open typeface. Never yellow on white.

00:01

The first second

Start rising immediately Moderate

Every best-performing emotional path began with the fastest possible rise, and a flat start was never optimal [Teixeira et al. 2012]. In a real YouTube field study, an ad that opened on a high-energy moment kept 73% of viewers at 5–10 seconds against 49% for a calm opening [Belanche et al. 2017].

Surprise first, joy to hold Moderate

Surprise concentrated gaze about three times more than joy and protected most at the very start. The rate at which joy rose did most to stop people leaving [Teixeira et al. 2012]. Keep the surprise about the subject: in forced pre-roll ads, surprise raised skipping [Campbell et al. 2017]. Eyes go to whatever is surprising: 72% of eye movements landed on spots a model scored as surprising, and the same event shown again carried about a third of the surprise [Itti & Baldi 2009].

Never repeat the same visual trick in one video.

Start the motion, don't arrive mid-motion Test it

An object that starts moving grabs attention, but one already moving when the screen appears gives no advantage over a still one [Abrams & Christ 2003]. Objects growing toward the viewer capture attention, while receding ones and colour changes on their own don't [Franconeri & Simons 2003]. An object on a collision course with the viewer was found 3.5 times more efficiently than the same object heading slightly to one side [Lin et al. 2008]. An object that suddenly changes speed or direction also grabs attention [Pratt et al. 2010]. Moving pictures raised arousal over the same pictures held still [Detenber et al. 1998].

Try a still frame for about 0.3 s, then a centred push-in straight at the viewer.

No logo, no intro card Strong

Early branding raised zapping, skipping or avoidance in five separate studies [Teixeira et al. 2012] [Becker et al. 2022] [Teixeira et al. 2010] [Tellis et al. 2019] [Campbell et al. 2017]. Brief, small brand "pulses" beat both no branding and one long reveal [Teixeira et al. 2010].

No loud slam Moderate

A loud opening raised ad skipping [Campbell et al. 2017].

00:05

Front-load the first five seconds

The opening does half the work Moderate

Half of a 30-second ad's effect was delivered in its first 5 seconds, and a 7-second ad delivered 60% of a 30-second ad's recall [Varan et al. 2020]. This was measured under forced viewing.

Irritation is the swipe trigger Strong

Across 237,851 real TV viewings, irritation predicted zapping and a lack of enjoyment didn't [Becker et al. 2022].

Cut hype, exaggeration, clutter, shouting and hard sells before asking whether the video is exciting enough.

00:10–00:15

The danger zone

Plan a second rise before ten seconds Moderate

The risk of zapping rose to a peak at about 11–12 seconds [Teixeira et al. 2012]. Stopping rose at the start and again near the end [Woltman Elpers et al. 2003], and skipping had a second bump near 15 seconds [Jeon et al. 2022]. In real household TV data, the risk of switching away peaked about 10 seconds into an ad [Gustafson & Siddarth 2007].

the middle

Pacing

Peak, valley, peak Moderate

Repeated rises kept the most viewers, and saving the key moment for the finale was the worst path measured [Teixeira et al. 2012]. Alternating a tense beat with a warm one beat grouping them (liking 4.01 vs 3.41), though not for music content, where the effect wasn't significant [Berger et al. 2021].

One idea at a time Strong

Information made people stop watching, and high entertainment combined with dense information was the worst mix measured [Woltman Elpers et al. 2003]. A facts framing cut viewing time while a feelings framing raised it [Olney et al. 1991]. Informative posts earned fewer likes [Lee et al. 2018] [Dong et al. 2024]. Overload hit when fast cutting met dense information [Lang et al. 2007].

One fact per beat, told as a feeling, and never on top of a fast-cut stretch.

Match the cut pace to the content Moderate

Emotional messages were remembered best at slow-to-medium cutting, while calm messages tolerated fast cutting [Lang et al. 1999]. New angles on the same scene didn't overload anyone: up to 24 or more a minute, recognition and arousal both rose [Lang et al. 2000]. A burst of one shot per second reads as riveting but can't be sustained [Cutting et al. 2010]. Average Hollywood shots shrank from about 10 seconds to under 4 since 1935 [Cutting et al. 2011].

Get pace from new angles on the same scene, give a new scene or a dense fact time to land, and ease off at the emotional peak.

Set up each new shot Test it

About a third of a second after a cut, viewers' eyes land in the same place whether or not they understand what they're seeing. Only viewers who had the story so far drew the right conclusion from the new shot, and without it their gaze wandered longer [Loschky et al. 2015]. Viewers felt a new event begin where the action changed, not at an ordinary cut [Magliano & Zacks 2011].

Let the line before a cut set up the next shot. Keep the action continuous across cuts within a beat, and change it where the beat changes.

Breaks renew enjoyment Test it

A short break made an enjoyable experience enjoyable again (64.0 vs 37.9 in the final seconds), even though people predicted breaks would hurt [Nelson & Meyvis 2008]. It worked only for repetitive content: a fast, varied clip gained nothing from breaks [Nelson et al. 2009].

Try a brief drop in the music or a cut-away in a long stretch.

Move things together Test it

Crowded moving items were hard to follow, and staggering an animation rarely helped viewers keep track [Chevalier et al. 2014].

Animate related items together and keep moving items spaced.

Ease every move, and animate only what means something Moderate

Moves that start and end slowly were tracked most accurately [Dragicevic et al. 2011]. Animated transitions beat hard cuts for following a change, about one second long, and multi-step animations did worse [Heer & Robertson 2007]. Across 76 comparisons, animation beat still pictures only when the motion was the content; decorative motion did nothing [Höffler & Leutner 2007].

A few well-placed sound events Moderate

More voice changes, sound effects and music entrances raised recall from 56% to 76% with no overload at the levels tested [Potter & Choi 2006]. But what is said right after a voice change is remembered less (53% vs 64%) for about three seconds [Potter 2000].

Mark each beat with one sound event, and never land the key fact in the second right after it.

Keep a causal through-line Moderate

Scenes told in chronological, causal order held viewers more than the same scenes shuffled (d = 0.88) [Cohen et al. 2015].

the end

The peak and the end

The peak and the end decide the verdict Strong

Liking tracked the best moment and the final moment (r ≈ .95) more than all moments combined (r = .815). Raising the peak raised liking, lowering the end lowered it, adding neutral length changed nothing, and time after the peak hurt [Baumgartner et al. 1997]. Watch time tracked accumulated peaks, not average engagement (R² = .65) [Lin et al. 2022]. Only improving both the peak and the end beat the control on accuracy [Xie & Li 2026]. Adding a weaker item after the best one lowered the overall rating [Do et al. 2008]. Across film clips, the peak and the final moments predicted the overall rating while length barely mattered [Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993]. Across 10,717 viewers, how much they liked the ending drove their intention to share most [Shehu et al. 2016]. In a cold-water test, most people chose to repeat a longer trial because its extra ending was slightly less painful [Kahneman et al. 1993]. In webcam recordings of 12,230 ad viewings, the ads people liked showed a rising run of smiles and a high peak [McDuff et al. 2015].

Finish soon after your highest moment.

End on a positive emotion Strong

Positive content won on sharing or likes in all five studies here [Tellis et al. 2019] [Berger & Milkman 2012] [Guadagno et al. 2013] [Dong et al. 2024] [Lee et al. 2018]. High-energy positive emotions such as amusement, joy and awe had the edge in experiments [Berger 2011] [Berger & Milkman 2012]. Sadness lowered sharing [Berger & Milkman 2012] and raised skipping [Campbell et al. 2017].

Ask for comments at the end, not the start Indirect

A question in a post raised comments by 61% but cut likes by 15% [Lee et al. 2018].

on screen

Words: titles, hooks, captions

Say it plainly Moderate

Plain headlines beat every clickbait form on both curiosity and clicks, and questions were the weakest of all [Molina et al. 2021].

Name the subject, hold back the payoff Strong

Across 8,977 real headline tests, concrete wording won overall, though adding more concreteness only helped when the alternatives were vague [Aubin Le Quéré & Matias 2025].

One sad-tinged word can help; fear and outrage hurt Strong

In 12,448 headline experiments, each negative word raised clicks by 2.3%, driven by sadness. Fear and moral outrage lowered clicks [Robertson et al. 2023]. This measured clicks to read, not sharing or watching to the end.

Outcome first, cause last Moderate

Stating what happened and holding back why produced the most curiosity. Telling it in order produced the most suspense. Summarising everything up front was the least enjoyable [Knobloch et al. 2004].

Real uncertainty, positive payoff Moderate

Curiosity rose steadily with genuine uncertainty in six experiments. But when the answer looked like bad news, people were less willing to wait for it [van Lieshout et al. 2021a] [van Lieshout et al. 2021b].

Captions help viewers, not retention Indirect

Subtitles barely changed how much of a video people watched (Cramér's V = .01) [Pascual-Ferrá et al. 2024]. Keep them for accessibility and silent viewing, and don't count on them to hold people.

Animate the hard part; don't print the narration Moderate

On-screen text that repeated the voiceover didn't improve recall over no graphic and made viewers work harder, while an animated graphic doubled delayed recall. Graphics helped hard stories and hurt easy ones [Fox et al. 2004].

Show dense information with motion. Leave easy lines alone.

voiceover

Voice

Set the pace by how much a line carries Moderate

Listeners remembered most at about 170 words per minute on information-heavy lines and about 190 on light ones. Rates of 210–230 overloaded them, and slow speech on thin content did worst [Rodero 2015]. Fast speech cost listening effort [Müller et al. 2019] and slowed listeners' guesses about what came next [Yin et al. 2026]. Faster syllables cut attention and recall, while trimming pauses did not [Chattopadhyay et al. 2003]. Within the normal range, faster speech was more persuasive, up to about 190 words per minute [Miller et al. 1976].

Never speed up into a reveal, and never drag a light line. To save time, cut pauses, not syllable speed.

Start the key line high and fall Moderate

A high-to-low pitch within a sentence beat flat and rising delivery on recall (21.48% vs 17.64%) and on arousal [Rodero et al. 2017].

Mark where each beat starts and ends Moderate

Speakers close a topic on a falling pitch, open the next one higher, and pause longer between topics than within one. Listeners spot those boundaries from melody and pause alone [Swerts & Geluykens 1994].

Build, pause, accent Test it

Storytellers build to a climax with rising pitch and loudness, pause about a second, then put one short accent on the reveal. Listeners preferred the gentle end of these ranges, and full exaggeration sounded "too much" in synthetic speech [Theune et al. 2006].

Each emotion has its own sound Moderate

Elation has the highest pitch and loudness. Interest sits near neutral, pride is lower and quieter, and sadness is slower and softer [Banse & Scherer 1996]. High-energy positive voices sound smooth and bright, while a jagged, loud voice reads as anger [Goudbeek & Scherer 2010]. Listeners judged a voice's energy from its loudness and brightness, not its speed, and pushing the intensity made every emotion, joy included, sound less pleasant [Laukka et al. 2005]. Voice and music share one emotional code: joy is fast, fairly loud, high and varied, while sadness and tenderness are slow, quiet, low and falling [Juslin & Laukka 2003].

Direct the voice by emotion, not just "more energy".

Stress the new word Moderate

Putting the accent on the new information made sentences faster to understand, while flat delivery was slowest [Bock & Mazzella 1983]. A contrast accent on the key word raised later recognition from 77% to 91% [Fraundorf et al. 2010].

Keep the pitch natural and skip the creak Moderate

Raising pitch by 20% made speakers sound less truthful and more nervous, and slowing to 77% of normal speed was least persuasive [Apple et al. 1979]. A creaky "vocal fry" delivery lost to a normal voice in most comparisons [Anderson et al. 2014]. A falling ending, a lower pitch and a brisk pace all made speakers sound more confident [Guyer et al. 2018], and listeners heard pitch variation, not speed, as liveliness [Hincks 2005]. Lower-pitched versions of the same voice won votes and sounded more competent and trustworthy [Klofstad et al. 2012]. A confident voice is loud, wide-ranging and brisk and falls on the last word; an unconfident one is higher, slower, full of pauses and rises at the end, and listeners hear the difference from the first word [Jiang & Pell 2017]. When people tried to persuade, intonation did the most work, ahead of volume and speed [Mehrabian & Williams 1969].

Sound sure: natural or slightly low pitch, varied intonation, lines that end falling.

Use the most natural voice you can Moderate

A low-quality synthetic voice was trusted less and seen as less credible than a human one, while a high-quality synthetic voice was not significantly less credible [Chiou et al. 2020]. Modern synthetic voices matched humans on credibility but sounded less human and less engaging [Craig & Schroeder 2017]. Against an older machine voice, a human narrator taught better (transfer scores 4.2 vs 2.9) [Mayer et al. 2003]. A human voice also beat two synthetic ones on likability, trust and naturalness, and listeners said synthetic voices give themselves away through flat or too-perfect intonation, odd pauses, emotion that doesn't fit the words, and missing breaths [Kühne et al. 2020]. Listeners hear a personality even in a robotic voice, and liked it more when that personality matched the words [Nass & Lee 2001].

Use the best voice you have and spend the effort on expressive delivery. Keep natural breaths and pauses, and check that each line's emotion fits its words.

mix

Sound, music, humour and AI labels

A clean, rhythmic music bed Test it

Medium syncopation was liked most and made people want to move, and harsh dissonance flattened that effect [Matthews et al. 2019]. The most surprising music was liked least [Gold et al. 2023] [Gold et al. 2019], and pleasure peaked when a surprise followed a predictable setup [Cheung et al. 2019].

Music sets how the scene is read Moderate

The same ambiguous scene was read as friendly or threatening depending on the music, and viewers remembered details that matched the music's mood, including some that never appeared [Boltz 2001]. In radio ads, recognisable sung vocals raised attention and memory, while an instrumental bed did no better than silence [Allan 2006]. The same neutral face was liked more under warm music and less under tense music [Hoeckner et al. 2011], and a review of 24 studies found the effect strongest on ambiguous pictures and when the music starts first [Herget & A.-K. 2019]. Arousing music helped people remember an ad only when it fit the message, and no music at all matched or beat most musical versions [Kellaris et al. 1993]. Fitting music raised recall of radio ads, and mismatched music did no better than none [North et al. 2004]. Tempo set how energised listeners felt and major or minor mode set their mood, independently [Husain et al. 2002].

Choose the bed's tempo for energy and its mode for mood, and match both to what the voice is saying. A bed that doesn't fit is no better than silence.

Build musical peaks the way chills are built Test it

Listeners got chills on the same passages, and each one combined a soft-to-loud rise, a new instrument or voice entering, a widening range and a brief harmonic surprise. Familiarity with the music didn't matter [Guhn et al. 2007].

Light humour about the subject Moderate

Humour's biggest effect is on attention (r = .416 in a meta-analysis) [Eisend 2009]. It reduced ad skipping [Campbell et al. 2017] and earned likes [Lee et al. 2018], but read as irritating in some TV ads [Becker et al. 2022].

Faces help less than you'd think Indirect

Eyes reach a face within about a tenth of a second [Crouzet et al. 2010], but faces raised likes on one platform and made no difference on another [Bakhshi et al. 2014] [Li & Xie 2019]. An irrelevant face slowed people down by about 20 ms while they searched for something else [Langton et al. 2008], so a face competes with whatever else is meant to hold the eye.

An AI label doesn't sink a video; low quality does Indirect

Disclosing that a short video was AI-made had no significant overall effect on intention to engage. It mattered through how good the video looked [Chen et al. 2025].

total

Length

Shorter keeps more viewers Moderate

Longer ads were skipped more [Campbell et al. 2017]. A 58-second cut beat a 115-second cut at every point viewers dropped off [Pascual-Ferrá et al. 2024], and length was the one design feature that predicted how much of a video people watched [Doherty 2022]. Across 6.9 million video sessions, median viewing topped out around 6 minutes however long the video ran [Guo et al. 2014], and 15-second ads were switched away from about two-thirds as often as 30-second ones [Gustafson & Siddarth 2007]. In TV recall data, 15-second ads got 81% of a 30-second ad's recall with the same correct branding [Newstead & Romaniuk 2010].

Make it as short as the story allows. Every extra second must build toward the peak.

the idea

Choosing the subject

Familiar subject, fresh execution Moderate

Familiar brands reduced zapping while familiar ads raised it [Teixeira et al. 2012] [Woltman Elpers et al. 2003]. Feeling that a subject was relevant drove the peaks that drove watch time [Lin et al. 2022].

Distinctive within its category Moderate

Hit songs were "optimally differentiated": close to their genre, but not too close [Askin & Mauskapf 2017]. Songs whose lyrics stood out from their genre ranked higher [Berger & Packard 2018]. Novel beats bizarre [Olney et al. 1991], and creative ads were zapped less [Becker et al. 2022].
